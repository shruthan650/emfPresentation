Organization design · classroom presentation

How should an organization be structured?

Organizational structure is the formal system of task and reporting relationships that controls, coordinates, and motivates employees so they work together toward the organization’s goals. It acts as the operating architecture of a business, deciding how information flows, how authority is used, how accountability is kept, and how resources are shared across teams.

  • Authority
  • Communication
  • Decision-making
  • Coordination
  • Accountability

How structures evolved

Organizational design has changed as markets, technology, and the nature of work have changed.

Classical management

Max Weber (Theory of Bureaucracy) and Henri Fayol (Administrative Theory) stressed rigid hierarchy, clear scalar chains, specialization, and central control. This produced the traditional Tall structure.

Mid-to-late 20th century

Peter Drucker and Henry Mintzberg saw that bureaucratic rigidity slowed decisions, blocked innovation, and created silos. This led to the Flat structure, built on autonomy and wider spans of control.

Globalization

Firms began unbundling functions to specialist vendors coordinated by a central core, forming the Network structure.

Digital era

Cloud tools and remote collaboration made it possible to work across physical, geographic, and time-zone boundaries, creating the Virtual structure.

How we compare structures

Management literature evaluates every structure using five core dimensions. Watch for these throughout the 3D presentation.

Formalization

How far rules, procedures, job descriptions, and communication are written down and standardized.

Centralization

Where decision-making authority sits within the hierarchy.

Span of control

The number of direct reports a manager supervises.

Chain of command

The continuous line of authority from the top to the lowest level, showing who reports to whom.

Departmentalization

How jobs are grouped together: functional, divisional, matrix, or network-based.

The four structures at a glance

Each model distributes authority, communication, and coordination differently, and each comes with its own trade-offs.

Tall · hierarchical

Many management levels, narrow spans of control (3–6 reports), a strict chain of command, and close supervision. Common in government, military, banking, and large-scale manufacturing.

Flat · horizontal

Few management tiers, wide spans of control (10–30+ reports), decentralized decisions, and direct communication. Common in startups, creative agencies, and SMEs.

Network · modular

A small core hub keeps its core competencies and coordinates outsourced partners through contracts and SLAs. Example: Nike outsourcing manufacturing.

Virtual · boundaryless

A distributed, cloud-native team working across locations and time zones, judged on results rather than presence. Common in software, SaaS, and consulting firms.
